Note: the last line uses ọ dịghị mma (not mma) and is not confirmed. Replace it with the parent's version if needed.

**Culture notes (custom and respect)**
- WV says that in Igbo society it is expected to use special greetings with anyone significantly older than you. In villages, a younger person is also expected to greet every elder they meet during the day.
- Titles and honorifics go before a name, such as Mazị or Daa (WV). Calling an adult "nwá" (child), "nwóké" (male) or "nwaanyị" (female) can sound patronising (WV).
- Titles such as nze, ọzọ and ichie are community-specific. NO and WV describe them differently. Ask a local speaker before using them about a real person.
- Kola is offered to guests with a prayer (NKN, curriculum). The host usually starts the kola ceremony and the eldest or highest-status person present often breaks the kola (HT, search summary). Practice varies by community.
- Ndo is a word for sympathy. It is not always used as a true apology (WV).

**Doubts for the parent (custom and respect)**
1. Ichie: NO says "a title holder; a noble rank" (and "lord"), but WV says "literally elder" and gives it as the way to address male elders. Which is right for learners?
2. Nze: WV says northern Igboland only; NO says "highest chieftaincy in Igbo tradition". Which should the app teach?
3. Kneeling or bowing to elders: no source here confirmed an Igbo rule. The Guardian mention was of hand gestures (search summary). The brainbound greetings guide returned HTTP 403, so it was not read. Please confirm whether any gesture (kneeling, bowing, shaking hands) should be taught.
4. Omenala and odinala: neither is in NO. WV uses omenala only inside a traditional-marriage phrase. Is "custom" the right gloss, and which spelling is standard?
5. ụkpụrụ: NO gives "trace; pattern; lineage", not "custom". Do not use it for custom unless you say otherwise.
6. Nna anyị / Nne anyị: no source found. Is this the usual respectful way to say "our father/mother"?
7. Who breaks the kola: sources say the host or the eldest/highest-status person (HT, search summary). Is this how your family does it?
8. Ndị okenye: WV says "adults"; NO says "elders". Which should the app use?

**Culture notes (life events)**
- Traditional marriage (igba nkwu) has several stages. The main ones are knocking on the door (ịkụ aka), the bride-price negotiation (ime ego), and the wine-carrying day (WPT). Many families also have a church or civil wedding. Practice varies by community.
- Naming: the naming ceremony is usually on the 7th to 12th day after birth. The paternal grandparents officiate. The ceremony starts with kola nut and a libation, and the father gives the name (UMU). Many families now name the baby at birth instead (UMU).
- Omugwo: an older female relative (often the paternal grandmother for the mother) helps the new mother and cares for the baby (NO, OMG, POST). The exact length of time varies.
- Christmas: many Igbo families travel home for Christmas. New clothes, plenty of food, and masquerades are common (XMAS). The Anambra government promotes the season as "Onwa Dezemba".
- Funerals: a funeral usually has a wake, the burial, and a reception. Some families hold a later second burial (ikwa ozu, BUR). Condolence visits and gifts are part of the custom. One state law limited condolence visits to one day (BUR, Daily Trust search summary).
- August meeting: an annual women's gathering in August or September, mainly for married women from the towns and villages (AUG, search summaries).
